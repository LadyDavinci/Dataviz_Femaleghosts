Finding 01 — the reversal

The author and the face are two different genders.

Across a century's worth of possession stories, curses, and hauntings, the entity people imagine — the thing named in the film's own mythology — skews male. The body the audience actually watches scream, crawl, and kill skews female.

Finding 02 — how she's made scary

Three ways the vessel is built differently than the author.

If the body doing the killing is more often female, what does the writing give her to work with? Three patterns hold consistently across the sample.

01 / 03

She doesn't carry a weapon. He does.

Only 1 of 51 female antagonists has a signature weapon. Nearly half of male antagonists do. When the threat is female, the body itself is treated as the instrument — no blade required.

Share of antagonists with a fixed, signature weapon
02 / 03

She's younger. He's older.

Median apparent age: 25 for female antagonists, 38 for male. A third of female antagonists read as under 20 — roughly double the rate for male ones. The vessel skews toward less power in the story it's trapped inside.

Apparent / canonical age of every antagonist (capped at 120 yrs — a few immortal entities run into the thousands)
03 / 03

Nearly half the time, she isn't even herself.

This is the sharpest gap. I coded every physical antagonist by whether the original person would actually do what's on screen — or whether an outside force has overridden them. 47% of female antagonists are possessed, inhabited, or otherwise not themselves — versus 22% of male antagonists, who are far more often just being who they are.

Before you take this further

Where this could be wrong.

Case notes
  • Sample is 100 films, supernatural/paranormal horror only — no slasher comparison exists in this dataset.
  • "Acting as self vs. overridden" is an interpretive judgment call made film-by-film, not a column in the source data. A handful of cases (Hellraiser, Christine, Constantine) are genuinely borderline.
  • No literal box-office dollar figures — only "trendsetter" vs. "mid-section" revenue tier labels, so revenue claims are directional, not exact.
  • Gender coding collapses complex or dual entities (e.g. "Male / Female-presenting") into simplified categories for comparison.
